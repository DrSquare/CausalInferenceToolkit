## The central question

Prediction asks:

- What outcome do we expect after observing features?
- Example: How many users will use ChatGPT in August 2026?
- Statistical target: `E[Y | X = x]` or a full predictive distribution.

Causal inference asks:

- What outcome would change if we intervened?
- Example: How many incremental users use ChatGPT because a new coworking feature was launched?
- Causal target: `E[Y | do(T = 1)] - E[Y | do(T = 0)]`, or a related potential-outcome contrast.

The difference is not the algorithm. The difference is the estimand and the assumptions that make it identifiable.

---

## Prediction vs. causal inference

| Topic | Prediction | Causal inference |
|---|---|---|
| Basic question | What is likely to happen? | What would happen under an intervention? |
| Typical target | `E[Y | X = x]`, `P(Y | X = x)` | `E[Y(t1) - Y(t0)]`, `E[Y | do(T=t)]` |
| Data needed | Predictive signal | Credible variation in treatment |
| Main risk | Poor generalization | Biased causal interpretation |
| Role of ML | Flexible prediction | Nuisance estimation, heterogeneity, policy targeting |

Important correction: prediction models are not always deterministic or black-box, and causal models are not automatically interpretable. Causal interpretation comes from the design and assumptions, not from using a particular model class.

## A minimal causal vocabulary

- Unit: the entity receiving treatment, such as a person, store, product, market, or time period.
- Treatment `T`: the action, exposure, policy, price, ad, or feature launch.
- Outcome `Y`: the business, clinical, or policy metric of interest.
- Covariates `X`: pre-treatment variables used for adjustment, matching, heterogeneity, or diagnostics.
- Potential outcome `Y(1)`: outcome the same unit would have under treatment.
- Potential outcome `Y(0)`: outcome the same unit would have under control.
- Individual treatment effect: `Y(1) - Y(0)`, usually unobservable for one unit.

We observe one realized outcome per unit, not both potential outcomes. This is the fundamental missing-data problem of causal inference.

---

## Common estimands

| Estimand | Meaning | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| ATE | Average treatment effect for the full population | Overall policy or product impact |
| ATT | Average treatment effect on treated units | Effect on those who actually received treatment |
| CATE | Conditional average treatment effect, `E[Y(1)-Y(0) | X=x]` | Personalization, targeting, segmentation |
| LATE/CACE | Local average effect for compliers affected by an instrument | Instrumental variables |
| Event-time ATT | Effect by time since adoption | Difference-in-differences with staggered rollout |

Always name the estimand before choosing the estimator.

---

## Identification, estimation, inference, decision

These are separate steps.

1. Identification: Under what assumptions is the causal effect knowable from the observed data?
2. Estimation: Which statistical or ML method estimates that identified quantity?
3. Inference: How uncertain is the estimate?
4. Decision: Is the estimated effect large enough, reliable enough, and relevant enough to act on?

Many errors happen when a powerful estimator is used before the identification question is settled.

## Learning paradigms in context

| Paradigm | Main object | Example question | Causal caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Supervised learning | Predictive function or distribution | Who will churn? | Churn drivers are not necessarily intervention levers. |
| Generative AI | Distribution over outputs | What text/image/code is likely? | Generated explanations are not causal evidence. |
| Reinforcement learning | Policy maximizing expected reward | Which action should an agent take next? | Off-policy evaluation needs identification assumptions. |
| Causal inference | Effect of an intervention | What happens if we change price? | Requires design, assumptions, and diagnostics. |

## Generative AI in one slide

An autoregressive language model estimates a distribution over next tokens given previous tokens:

`P(token_t | token_1, ..., token_{t-1})`

The generation process may look deterministic if decoding uses a fixed rule, but the model itself represents a probability distribution. This is still prediction, not causal inference.

Generative AI can help causal work by drafting code, summarizing assumptions, or simulating examples. It does not replace a research design.

---

## Reinforcement learning in one slide

At each step:

- The agent observes state or observation `O_t`.
- The agent chooses action `A_t`.
- The environment returns reward `R_{t+1}` and next observation `O_{t+1}`.

Reinforcement learning optimizes sequential decisions. Causal inference enters when we ask what would have happened under a different policy, especially from logged data generated by an old policy.

## Two complementary causal languages

| Language | Core idea | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Potential outcomes | Compare `Y(1)` vs. `Y(0)` for the same unit conceptually | Clear estimands, experiments, matching, DiD, IV, RDD |
| Graphical/structural causal models | Represent causal relations and interventions using graphs and structural equations | Clarifies adjustment, mediators, colliders, transportability, counterfactual logic |

They are not competing religions. For applied work, use both: define the estimand in potential-outcome terms and use a DAG or structural story to defend the assumptions.

## Causal inference toolkit map

| Area | Question | Typical data | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Causal discovery | What might cause what? | Observational data, sometimes time series | Strong assumptions; not a substitute for domain knowledge |
| Causal representation learning | What latent causal variables explain complex data? | Images, text, audio, sensor data | Often non-identifiable without structure or interventions |
| Causal effect estimation | How much does an intervention change an outcome? | Tabular, panel, experiment, quasi-experiment | Identification assumptions must be explicit |

This deck focuses on causal effect estimation.

## Randomized experiments

Randomization is powerful because treatment is assigned independently of potential outcomes:

`T independent of (Y(1), Y(0))`

In product or pricing settings, experiments can still be hard because of:

- Opportunity cost and risk of bad treatment arms
- Large action spaces across products, prices, markets, and timing
- Interference: one product or customer treatment can affect another
- Legal, fairness, brand, and operational constraints
- Network or marketplace spillovers

Experiments are not always impossible at scale, but the unit of randomization and guardrails must match the business context.

---

## Observational causal inference

Observational data were not generated by a randomized design.

For demand estimation, historical prices may be high exactly when demand is high, because managers raise prices in peak seasons or when inventory is scarce. A naive regression of sales on price can therefore show positive price elasticity even when the true causal price effect is negative.

The problem is not "too little data." The problem is whether the data contain credible variation in treatment after accounting for confounding.

---

## Demand example: why price can look positively related to sales

Imagine two seasons:

- High season: demand is high, prices are high, sales are high.
- Low season: demand is low, prices are low, sales are low.

Across seasons, price and sales are positively correlated. Within a comparable season, raising price may reduce unit sales.

The omitted variable is seasonality or latent demand. A causal design must separate price variation from demand shocks.

## Common observational problems

| Problem | Description | Typical tools |
|---|---|---|
| Observed confounding | Pre-treatment `X` affects both `T` and `Y` | Regression adjustment, matching, propensity scores, DML |
| Unmeasured confounding | Unobserved `U` affects both `T` and `Y` | IV, RDD, DiD, sensitivity analysis, experiments |
| Reverse causality | Expected `Y` changes treatment assignment | Lagged policies, IV, structural assignment model |
| Selection bias | Treated and control units are not comparable | Design restrictions, matching, weighting, trimming |
| Interference | One unit's treatment affects another unit's outcome | Cluster designs, marketplace models, spillover analysis |
| Limited overlap | Some units have almost no chance of treatment or control | Restrict target population, trimming, redesign |

---

## Confounding from observed covariates

Classic setup:

- `X -> T`: covariates influence treatment assignment.
- `X -> Y`: the same covariates influence the outcome.
- We want the effect `T -> Y`.

If all confounders are observed, and each unit has a positive probability of receiving either treatment, adjustment methods can identify causal effects.

The key assumptions are:

- Conditional exchangeability: `(Y(1), Y(0)) independent of T | X`
- Positivity/overlap: `0 < P(T=1 | X=x) < 1`
- Consistency and well-defined treatment

---

## Unmeasured confounding

If an unobserved variable `U` affects both treatment and outcome, ordinary regression, matching, and propensity score methods cannot remove the bias by themselves.

Examples:

- Latent demand affects both price and sales.
- Ability affects both education and wages.
- Health severity affects both treatment choice and mortality.

Possible responses:

- Find a stronger design: IV, DiD, RDD, natural experiment, randomized encouragement.
- Collect missing confounders.
- Use sensitivity analysis to quantify how strong hidden confounding would need to be.
- Narrow the claim if identification is weak.

---

## Simultaneity and reverse causality

Suppose advertising is increased after sales decline or before expected demand spikes. Then advertising is partly a response to the outcome process.

A high `R^2` regression of sales on advertising can still be causally wrong.

Ask:

- Who decided treatment assignment?
- What information did they use?
- Was the rule based on expected outcomes?
- Did the treatment respond to previous outcome shocks?
- Is there variation that is unrelated to expected outcomes?

---

## Signal, noise, and useful variation

A 30 percent before/after increase may be meaningful or meaningless depending on variation.

Do not rely only on means. Report:

- Baseline variance
- Standard errors or confidence intervals
- Number of independent units, not only number of rows
- Whether variation in treatment is plausibly exogenous
- Whether treatment changes are large enough to estimate the effect

More rows do not guarantee more causal information. Repeated copies of the same decision rule often add little identifying variation.

---

## Data structures and sources of variation

| Data type | Units | Time periods | Main variation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Cross-section | Many | One | Across units |
| Repeated cross-section | Many, not necessarily same units | Many | Across groups and time |
| Time series | One or few | Many | Within unit over time |
| Panel/longitudinal | Many same units | Many | Within unit over time plus across units |

Panel data are often "large N, small T", but not always. The method should follow the assignment process, not only the data shape.

---

## Reduced-form vs. structural econometrics

| Approach | Goal | Strength | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reduced-form causal inference | Estimate a well-defined causal effect using design-based variation | Transparent estimand and assumptions | Limited extrapolation outside observed variation |
| Structural econometrics | Model behavior, constraints, equilibrium, and counterfactual policies | Can simulate counterfactuals beyond observed choices | Stronger model assumptions and misspecification risk |

Reduced-form assumptions are not always "weak" or fully testable. Often we can test implications, not the identifying assumption itself.

---

## Method selection by identification problem

| If the problem is... | Consider... | Core assumption |
|---|---|---|
| Treated and control differ on observed covariates | Matching, propensity score weighting, regression, DML | No unobserved confounding conditional on `X` |
| Treatment is endogenous but there is a credible nudge | Instrumental variables | Instrument relevance, independence, exclusion, monotonicity |
| A policy starts for treated units but not controls | Difference-in-differences | Parallel trends for untreated potential outcomes |
| Treatment is assigned at a cutoff | Regression discontinuity | Continuity around cutoff, no precise manipulation |
| One treated aggregate unit has many pre-periods | Synthetic control | Weighted controls can reproduce untreated counterfactual path |
| Treatment effects vary by user/store/product | CATE, meta-learners, causal forests | Same identification assumptions as the base design |

## Matching

Goal: compare treated units to similar untreated units.

Matching can reduce model dependence and make treated/control comparisons more transparent. It is most useful when:

- Confounders are observed before treatment.
- Treated and control units overlap in covariate space.
- There are enough controls similar to treated units.

Matching does not solve unmeasured confounding. It also does not guarantee balance. Always check balance after matching.

---

## Matching choices

Common choices:

- Exact matching: same values on selected covariates.
- Nearest-neighbor matching: smallest distance between covariates.
- Mahalanobis matching: distance scaled by covariance of covariates.
- Propensity score matching: match on estimated probability of treatment.
- Calipers: reject matches that are too far away.
- Matching with or without replacement.
- 1:1, many-to-one, variable-ratio, or full matching.

Design principle: decide the matching rule before looking at outcomes.

---

## Matching diagnostics

After matching, inspect:

- Standardized mean differences before and after matching.
- Distributional balance, not just means.
- Propensity score overlap.
- Number of treated units dropped due to no good match.
- Whether the estimand changed from ATE to ATT or to a trimmed population.

Rules of thumb such as standardized mean difference below 0.1 are useful, but they are not proof of causal validity.

---

## Propensity scores

The propensity score is:

`e(X) = P(T = 1 | X)`

Under conditional exchangeability, the true propensity score is a balancing score. Units with the same true propensity score have the same distribution of observed covariates in expectation.

Practical caution:

- Estimated propensity scores can be misspecified.
- Finite samples can still be imbalanced.
- Extreme scores create unstable weights.
- A good propensity model predicts treatment assignment, not the outcome directly.

---

## Propensity score workflows

Common workflows:

- Matching on propensity score
- Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW)
- Overlap weighting
- Stratification/subclassification
- Doubly robust estimators such as AIPW/TMLE

For business audiences, IPTW can be explained as reweighting the sample to create a pseudo-population where treatment is less confounded by observed covariates.

When overlap is poor, the honest answer may be: this dataset does not support the target causal question for the full population.

---

## Instrumental variables

An instrument `Z` is a source of variation in treatment `T` that is plausibly unrelated to unobserved determinants of outcome `Y`, except through treatment.

Example pattern:

`Z -> T -> Y`, with hidden `U -> T` and `U -> Y`.

IV is useful when treatment is endogenous and a credible nudge exists, such as:

- Random encouragement to adopt a behavior
- Eligibility rules
- Lottery assignment
- Distance or exposure measures, when defensible
- Supply shocks or policy rules, when defensible

"When defensible" matters. Many proposed instruments fail on exclusion or independence.

---

## IV assumptions

For a basic binary-instrument LATE interpretation:

1. Relevance: `Z` changes treatment probability.
2. Independence: `Z` is as-if randomly assigned conditional on covariates.
3. Exclusion restriction: `Z` affects `Y` only through `T`.
4. Monotonicity: no defiers; the instrument does not push some units in the opposite direction.
5. SUTVA/no interference: one unit's instrument does not affect another unit's outcome.

Important correction: exclusion does not mean "`Z` is independent of `Y` given `T`." Conditioning on treatment can be misleading. The claim is about potential outcomes or the structural error, not ordinary conditional independence after conditioning on treatment.

---

## 2SLS in plain language

Two-stage least squares estimates the part of treatment variation explained by the instrument, then relates that instrument-induced treatment variation to the outcome.

Stage 1:

`T = alpha0 + alpha1 Z + gamma'X + v`

Stage 2:

`Y = beta0 + beta1 T_hat + delta'X + error`

`beta1` is not automatically the ATE. With a binary valid instrument and monotonicity, it is a local average treatment effect for compliers. With continuous or multiple instruments, interpretation depends on the model and weighting.

Use software that computes valid IV standard errors. Do not manually run two OLS stages and use the second-stage OLS standard errors.

---

## IV diagnostics and caveats

Check:

- First-stage strength: weak instruments can badly bias estimates and inflate uncertainty.
- Balance of observed covariates across instrument values.
- Overidentification tests when multiple instruments exist, while remembering they do not prove validity.
- Sensitivity to covariates, sample restrictions, and alternative instruments.
- Whether the LATE population is relevant to the business or policy decision.

Exclusion and independence are mainly defended with design knowledge, not with a single statistical test.

---

## IV examples

Education and wages:

- Treatment: years of schooling.
- Outcome: wages.
- Candidate instrument: growing up near a four-year college.
- Concern: college proximity may be correlated with local labor markets, family background, or neighborhood resources.

Smoking during pregnancy:

- Treatment: smoking.
- Outcome: birthweight.
- Instrument: randomized encouragement to stop smoking.
- Interpretation: effect for people whose smoking behavior changed because of the encouragement.

Distance to specialty care:

- Treatment: receiving specialty care.
- Outcome: mortality or health.
- Instrument: differential travel time.
- Concern: geography may affect outcomes through other channels.

## Difference-in-differences

Canonical two-group, two-period DiD:

`Effect = (Y_treated_after - Y_treated_before) - (Y_control_after - Y_control_before)`

The design compares changes, not levels. Treated and control groups may start at different levels, but the untreated potential outcomes must have followed parallel trends.

Parallel trends is a counterfactual assumption: in the absence of treatment, treated units would have changed like control units.

---

## Parallel trends

With multiple pre-periods, plot event-study coefficients or group means before treatment.

What pre-trend checks can do:

- Reveal clear violations before treatment.
- Build credibility if trends are stable and similar.
- Identify anticipation effects.

What they cannot do:

- Prove that post-treatment counterfactual trends would have been parallel.
- Fix selection into treatment timing.
- Guarantee validity when treatment effects vary across cohorts.

---

## DiD regression

Canonical 2x2 regression:

`Y_it = alpha + beta Treat_i + gamma Post_t + tau (Treat_i * Post_t) + error_it`

Here `tau` is the DiD estimate.

For panels, include unit and time fixed effects when appropriate:

`Y_it = alpha_i + lambda_t + tau D_it + error_it`

Use clustered standard errors at the treatment-assignment level when outcomes are serially correlated or treatment is assigned by group.

---

## Staggered DiD

When units adopt treatment at different times, a simple two-way fixed-effects coefficient can be misleading if treatment effects differ by cohort or over event time.

Modern DiD estimators focus on group-time effects:

`ATT(g,t) = E[Y_t(1) - Y_t(0) | first treated in period g]`

Then they aggregate those effects into meaningful summaries:

- Overall ATT
- Dynamic/event-study effects
- Cohort-specific effects
- Calendar-time effects

Use never-treated or not-yet-treated controls carefully, and state which comparison group is used.

---

## Regression discontinuity

RDD uses a cutoff rule:

- Running variable: assignment score, age, income, test score, date, distance, etc.
- Cutoff: threshold for treatment eligibility or probability jump.
- Identification: units just below and just above the cutoff are comparable.

Sharp RDD:

- Treatment changes deterministically at the cutoff.

Fuzzy RDD:

- Treatment probability jumps at the cutoff, but compliance is imperfect.
- The estimand is local to compliers near the cutoff.

---

## RDD diagnostics

Check:

- No precise manipulation of the running variable around the cutoff.
- Continuity of pre-treatment covariates around the cutoff.
- Sensitivity to bandwidth and polynomial choices.
- Local nature of the estimand.
- Whether the cutoff population is relevant to the decision.

RDD is credible near the cutoff. It is not a license to extrapolate far from the threshold.

---

## Synthetic control

Synthetic control constructs a weighted combination of control units to approximate the treated unit's pre-treatment outcome path.

Useful when:

- There is one or a small number of treated aggregate units.
- Many pre-treatment periods are available.
- A weighted control path can closely match the treated pre-period path.

Caution:

- It is not simply a "better version of matching."
- It is a panel-data design with its own assumptions.
- Poor pre-treatment fit weakens credibility.

---

## Double/debiased machine learning

DML combines:

- Flexible ML for nuisance functions, such as outcome models and propensity/treatment models.
- Orthogonal scores that reduce first-order sensitivity to nuisance estimation error.
- Cross-fitting to reduce overfitting bias.

DML is useful when confounding is high-dimensional or nonlinear but observed.

DML does not discover causality. It estimates a specified causal estimand under identification assumptions such as unconfoundedness or valid instruments.

---

## DML intuition

For a partially linear model:

`Y = theta T + g(X) + error`

`T = m(X) + residual_T`

DML:

1. Predict outcome from covariates: `g_hat(X)`.
2. Predict treatment from covariates: `m_hat(X)`.
3. Residualize: `Y - g_hat(X)` and `T - m_hat(X)`.
4. Estimate the treatment effect using an orthogonal score.
5. Use cross-fitting so each observation is scored by nuisance models trained on other folds.

This protects the final estimate from some regularization and overfitting bias, but only under the required assumptions and rates.

---

## DML variants

| Variant | Treatment/outcome setup | Typical target |
|---|---|---|
| PLR | Continuous or binary treatment with partially linear effect | ATE-like slope |
| IRM | Binary treatment with flexible potential-outcome models and propensity | ATE or ATT |
| PLIV | Endogenous treatment with instruments | IV-style causal parameter |
| DiD-DML | Panel/repeated data with DiD structure | ATT under DiD assumptions |
| RDD-DML | RDD with ML nuisance adjustment | Local effect at cutoff |

Do not use a single "DML recipe" for every design. Match the score to the identification strategy.

---

## DML-IV

DML-IV extends IV ideas by using ML to estimate nuisance functions while preserving an orthogonal moment for the treatment effect.

High-level flow:

1. Model outcome, treatment, and instrument-related nuisance functions using cross-fitting.
2. Use residualized or orthogonalized variation tied to the instrument.
3. Estimate the causal parameter with an IV-style moment.

The same IV assumptions still matter:

- Relevance
- Independence
- Exclusion
- Monotonicity or model-specific interpretation

ML does not rescue a bad instrument.

## Heterogeneous treatment effects

Average effects can hide important variation.

Examples:

- A feature increases engagement for new users but not power users.
- A price discount helps high-margin products but cannibalizes low-margin products.
- A medication works differently by age or baseline severity.

CATE asks:

`tau(x) = E[Y(1) - Y(0) | X=x]`

HTE methods estimate effect variation, not just outcome variation. They still rely on the base design assumptions.

---

## HTE methods

| Method | Idea | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| S-learner | One outcome model with treatment as a feature | May understate effects if treatment signal is weak |
| T-learner | Separate models for treated and control outcomes | Can be noisy with imbalance |
| X-learner | Impute treatment effects and combine | Useful with treatment imbalance |
| Doubly robust learner | Combines outcome and propensity models | Needs careful nuisance estimation |
| Causal forest / GRF | Forest targeted to treatment-effect heterogeneity | Requires sufficient overlap and honest inference |
| Uplift models | Target incremental response for campaigns | Evaluation needs randomized or identified data |

HTE estimates are easy to overinterpret. Validate them with held-out policy value, rank metrics, or experiments when possible.

---

## Meta-learners: S-, T-, and X-Learner

Meta-learners estimate the CATE `tau(x) = E[Y(1) - Y(0) | X=x]` by wiring ordinary supervised regressors ("base learners") together. They are a *recipe*, not a model, so any regressor can be plugged in. They still require the base design's identifying assumptions (unconfoundedness and overlap for the observational case).

| Learner | How it works | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S-Learner** ("Single") | Fit one model `mu(X, T)` on pooled data with treatment `T` as an extra feature; `tau(x) = mu(x, 1) - mu(x, 0)`. | Simplest; a single model; naturally shrinks CATE toward 0 when there is genuinely no effect. | The lone model can wash out a weak treatment signal, biasing CATE toward 0 when `T` competes with strong controls. |
| **T-Learner** ("Two") | Fit two separate models, `mu_1(X)` on the treated and `mu_0(X)` on the controls; `tau(x) = mu_1(x) - mu_0(x)`. | Each arm modelled with full flexibility; no shared-form restriction; easy to reason about. | No borrowing of strength across arms; high variance and unstable with imbalanced arms or small treated groups. |
| **X-Learner** ("Cross") | Two-stage refinement of the T-learner: impute individual effects by crossing each arm's model onto the other arm, fit a second-stage effect model per arm, then combine using the propensity score as the weight. | Efficient under treatment imbalance; borrows strength across arms; propensity-weighted — usually the most robust. | Most moving parts (outcome + effect + propensity models); more to tune and to get wrong. |

Rule of thumb: reach for the **X-Learner** when treated and control groups are very different in size; the **S-Learner** is a fine, simple baseline; the **T-Learner** is easiest to explain but noisiest. In practice the S-learner often reports the smallest effect while T- and X-learners agree more closely.

Runnable example: `causal-inference-examples/examples/12_meta_learners.py` estimates all three on the 401(k) data and compares their CATE by income quartile against the causal forest (example 09).

## Causal forests and GRF

Causal forests are not ordinary random forests used for outcome prediction.

They build trees to learn local treatment effects, often using:

- Residualization or nuisance adjustment
- Honesty: separate data for choosing splits and estimating leaf effects
- Out-of-bag predictions
- Variance estimates for inference

Standard causal forests target CATE under unconfoundedness. Instrumental forests target conditional local average treatment effects when a valid instrument is available.

---

## Granger causality and time series caution

Granger causality means past values of one series improve prediction of another series, conditional on included lags.

It is a predictive notion, not automatically an intervention effect.

Use it carefully:

- Good for temporal precedence and forecasting diagnostics.
- Not sufficient for policy claims unless the time-series model and omitted-variable assumptions are credible.

---

## Causal workflow for applied projects

1. Write the intervention: "Set `T` from `t0` to `t1`."
2. Define the unit, time horizon, and outcome.
3. Choose the estimand: ATE, ATT, CATE, LATE, event-time ATT, local RDD effect.
4. Draw the assignment story or DAG.
5. Identify the required assumptions.
6. Check overlap, balance, pre-trends, first stage, or cutoff diagnostics as relevant.
7. Estimate with appropriate uncertainty.
8. Run sensitivity and robustness checks.
9. Translate the result into a decision with limitations.

## Common failure modes

- Treating correlation as causation because the model has high predictive accuracy.
- Using post-treatment variables as controls.
- Conditioning on colliders.
- Reporting ATE when poor overlap only supports a trimmed estimand.
- Claiming IV validity from first-stage strength alone.
- Treating pre-trend tests as proof of parallel trends.
- Using naive TWFE for staggered adoption with heterogeneous effects.
- Interpreting DML as a cure for unobserved confounding.
- Treating HTE segments as real without uncertainty or validation.
- Ignoring interference in marketplaces, social networks, and product portfolios.

## Demand estimation checklist

Before estimating a price, promotion, or feature-launch effect, ask:

- What was the assignment rule?
- Was treatment chosen using expected demand, inventory, competition, or customer value?
- Are treatment changes large and frequent enough?
- Is there overlap across comparable stores, products, customers, or weeks?
- Are seasonality and promotions observed?
- Are there spillovers across products or channels?
- Is the estimand short-run, long-run, direct, total, or equilibrium effect?
- Can we validate with an experiment, holdout, or natural experiment?

---

## Causal Demand Bench framing

Forecasting accuracy and causal fidelity are different goals.

A demand model can forecast well because it learns historical decision rules. It can still fail causal tests if it predicts what happened under the old policy rather than what would happen under a new price, promotion, or assortment policy.

A useful benchmark should evaluate:

- Predictive accuracy on observed outcomes.
- Direction and magnitude of known intervention effects.
- Robustness under confounding, limited overlap, and unobserved demand shocks.
- Policy value under counterfactual decisions.

## Closing thought

Causal inference is not a menu of estimators.

It is a workflow for making an intervention claim:

1. Define the causal question.
2. Defend the design.
3. Estimate the effect.
4. Quantify uncertainty.
5. State what the result does and does not support.
