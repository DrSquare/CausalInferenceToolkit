## Executive summary

I rewrote the deck as a clean Markdown seminar deck with slide separators. The original file appears to be a text export from slides, with mojibake encoding, broken equations, fragmented tables, and chart labels mixed into prose. Rather than making narrow copy edits to corrupted slide text, I preserved the teaching flow and rebuilt the content around clearer causal language, corrected assumptions, and current toolkit guidance.

The revised version is still introductory, but it is more rigorous about the distinction between identification and estimation, what each method can and cannot solve, and which assumptions are testable versus defended by design.

## High-impact factual corrections

| Original claim or implication | Correction made |
|---|---|
| Traditional ML is deterministic and causal inference is interpretable. | Prediction can be probabilistic or interpretable; causal interpretation comes from the estimand and design assumptions, not from the model class. |
| Causal effect estimation requires the causal graph to be known beforehand. | Identification requires explicit causal assumptions; DAGs help, but many designs can be expressed without a fully known graph. |
| Randomized pricing experiments are only feasible for a single product or very small subset. | Reframed as difficult and risky, not impossible; feasibility depends on randomization unit, guardrails, spillovers, and operational constraints. |
| Observational approaches suffer from lack of strategic exploration. | Reframed as a data-generating-process issue: some observational data have useful natural variation, while logged greedy policies often have limited overlap. |
| Matching and propensity score matching solve selection bias. | Clarified that they address observed confounding only under exchangeability and overlap; they do not handle unobserved confounding. |
| Propensity score matching ensures covariate balance and avoids the curse of dimensionality. | Clarified that the true propensity score is a balancing score in expectation; estimated finite-sample scores require balance diagnostics and can fail with poor overlap. |
| "No good Python packages yet" for matching. | Updated the toolkit guidance: R remains mature for matching diagnostics, but Python workflows exist through CausalML, DoWhy, scikit-learn/statsmodels, and related packages. |
| Exclusion restriction was described as `Z` independent of `Y` given `T`. | Corrected: exclusion means `Z` affects `Y` only through `T`; conditioning on `T` can be misleading. IV validity is about potential outcomes or structural errors. |
| 2SLS coefficient is the causal effect / CACE generally. | Clarified that 2SLS is not automatically ATE; with binary valid IV and monotonicity it has a LATE/CACE interpretation for compliers. |
| IV examples were framed as "solutions." | Reframed as candidate instruments that require relevance, independence, exclusion, and monotonicity arguments. |
| Multiple pre-periods can test the parallel trends assumption. | Clarified that pre-trends can falsify or support credibility but cannot prove post-treatment counterfactual parallel trends. |
| Staggered DiD was presented without warning about naive TWFE. | Added modern warning: two-way fixed effects can be biased with heterogeneous or dynamic effects; group-time ATT estimators are preferred. |
| RDD "jump" directly indicates causal effect. | Added continuity, no manipulation, bandwidth sensitivity, local estimand, and fuzzy/sharp distinction. |
| DML "clears background noise" and reveals pure causality. | Corrected: DML estimates specified causal parameters under identification assumptions using orthogonal scores and cross-fitting; it does not solve unobserved confounding. |
| DML recipe trained outcome models only within treated and untreated groups. | Reframed by design: PLR residualizes outcome and treatment using all relevant data; IRM-style binary treatment models use potential-outcome nuisance functions. |
| DML establishes causal relationships in high-dimensional data. | Corrected: DML estimates effects under a causal design; it does not discover causal relationships. |
| Causal forest was framed mainly as "GRF with IV." | Split standard causal forests under unconfoundedness from instrumental forests for conditional LATE with valid instruments. |
| Synthetic control was called a better version of matching. | Corrected: synthetic control is a panel-data weighted-counterfactual design, not universally better than matching. |
| Granger causality listed without caveat. | Added that Granger causality is predictive temporal precedence, not automatically an intervention effect. |

## Section-by-section notes

### Opening concepts

The revised deck opens with the distinction between `E[Y | X=x]` and intervention targets such as `E[Y | do(T=t)]` or potential-outcome contrasts. This is more rigorous than framing prediction as black-box mapping and causal inference as inherently interpretable.

### Learning paradigms

Generative AI and reinforcement learning were retained, but narrowed. The revised deck explains that language models represent conditional token distributions and that reinforcement learning connects to causal inference through off-policy evaluation and counterfactual policy questions.

### Causal traditions

The potential-outcomes and graphical/SCM traditions are now framed as complementary. The revised deck avoids assigning disciplines too rigidly to one tradition and emphasizes using both estimand definitions and DAG/assignment stories.

### Observational data problems

The revised deck distinguishes observed confounding, unmeasured confounding, reverse causality, selection bias, interference, and limited overlap. This makes the later method-choice section easier to understand and prevents overclaiming that one method "solves" causal inference.

### Matching and propensity scores

Matching is now framed as a design step under observed-confounding assumptions. The propensity score section explains balancing scores, overlap, finite-sample diagnostics, IPTW, overlap weighting, and doubly robust estimators.

### Instrumental variables

The IV section was the most important factual correction. The revised slides define relevance, independence, exclusion, monotonicity, and SUTVA. They also clarify that first-stage strength is necessary but not sufficient, and that standard errors must come from IV software rather than a manual two-OLS-stage workflow.

### Difference-in-differences

The revised DiD section separates canonical 2x2 DiD, pre-trend diagnostics, panel regression, clustered inference, and staggered adoption. It adds the modern group-time ATT framework for staggered rollouts.

### Regression discontinuity and synthetic control

The revised RDD section adds local identification, bandwidth sensitivity, manipulation checks, covariate continuity, and sharp/fuzzy distinctions. Synthetic control is now treated as its own panel-data design rather than a superior form of matching.

### Double/debiased ML and HTE

The revised DML section is less metaphorical and more precise. It explains nuisance functions, orthogonal scores, cross-fitting, PLR/IRM/PLIV variants, and the key limitation: DML does not create identification. HTE is presented as effect heterogeneity under the same base assumptions, not as ordinary predictive segmentation.
